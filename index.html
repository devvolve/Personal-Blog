About

Two degrees in computer science, and I still learn best by breaking things.

I'm a software engineer who spends most working hours on backend systems and LLM-driven workflows, and most free ones either reading model papers I probably should've read in school, or breaking a game prototype in some new and educational way.

I studied computer science end to end: a B.S. from UC San Diego, with a minor in computer science alongside it, and an M.S. from Cal Poly Pomona. What's kept me interested since graduating is less the theory and more the feedback loop — building something, shipping it, watching it fail in a way that teaches me something specific.

The degree got me in the room. The failures taught me the job.

This site is where I keep track of what I'm building and what I'm reading, mostly so I don't lose track of it myself.

Portfolio

A showcase of what I've built

Three projects, spanning language model tooling and game development.

Currently

prompt-lab

Python · FastAPI · React

A sandbox for testing prompt structures and comparing model outputs side by side — built because I got tired of copy-pasting into three browser tabs to compare one change.

tokenviz

Python · D3.js

A small visual tool that shows how a sentence gets broken into tokens, step by step. Started as a way to explain the idea to someone else, kept it because it's still useful to me.

pixel-forge

C++ · OpenGL

A tiny 2D game engine I keep rebuilding from scratch every time I learn something new about rendering. The game changes; the excuse to rebuild the engine doesn't.

Writing

Notes on models and games

Short pieces on what I'm learning, published when they're done, not before.

Building tokenviz forced me to actually sit with how models read text, instead of nodding along to the explanation. Notes on what surprised me, and the diagram that finally made it click.

None of my games ship. That used to bother me. Now I think of pixel-forge as a testbed for taste — a place to feel bad code before it reaches anything that matters.

Two years out of my master's, going back to primary sources on attention and retrieval felt slower and more useful than any course did. A short list of what I'd read first, knowing what I know now.
